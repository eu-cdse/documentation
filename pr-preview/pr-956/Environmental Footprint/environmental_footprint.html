    1. Environmental Impact of Copernicus Data Use

    1.1. Objectives of the activity

    The Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem (CDSE) provides free and open access to Earth Observation data used by researchers, public authorities, businesses, NGOs and citizens worldwide. In 2024, the ecosystem hosted more than 78 PB of data and served nearly 290,000 registered users. As CDSE usage continues to grow, understanding the environmental impact associated with data usage becomes increasingly important.

    Although digital data appears immaterial, every interaction relies on physical resources such as user devices, communication networks, datacentres and storage infrastructure. Accessing, processing, storing or sharing Earth Observation data therefore generates environmental impacts beyond the operation of satellites and CDSE services themselves.

    The objective of this study is not to discourage the use of Copernicus data. On the contrary, it aims to preserve the scientific, societal and operational value created by Earth Observation while identifying opportunities to avoid unnecessary environmental impacts.

    From platform operation to data usage

    In 2024, ESA assessed the environmental footprint of the Copernicus Ground Segment. While this first assessment quantified impacts associated with data transmission, computing and storage infrastructure, it provided only a preliminary estimate of end-user impacts due to limited visibility on actual user practices.

    The present study extends the scope of analysis by examining how users actually interact with Copernicus data. Through surveys, interviews and environmental modelling, the assessment evaluates what happens after data leaves the platform, including downloading, processing, storage and redistribution activities.

    This shift is important because environmental impacts are increasingly driven by usage behaviour rather than infrastructure alone. The same dataset may be streamed, downloaded, processed multiple times, retained for years or redistributed to other users, creating significantly different environmental footprints.

    1.2. Understanding CDSE Usage

    The Copernicus Data Space Ecosystem is a multi-access, cloud-based Earth Observation ecosystem. It replaced the former Sentinel Data Access Services and provides a unified environment in which users can discover, access, visualize and process Copernicus data.

    CDSE provides free, open and immediate access to the data repository for the Copernicus Sentinel missions managed by ESA. It supports users ranging from newcomers and individual users to researchers, public organisations and commercial users.

    Overview of Services

    Users can interact with Copernicus data through several complementary interfaces and services.

    Services Description
    Copernicus Browser The Copernicus Browser provides a graphical environment for searching, visualising and exploring Earth Observation data. It supports data discovery and visual inspection before users decide whether further processing or downloading is necessary.
    OData and S3 interfaces OData and S3 interfaces support programmatic data discovery and retrieval. The S3 interface enables direct access to complete files or specific parts of files and is designed for cloud-oriented applications.
    Sentinel Hub Sentinel Hub supports streamlined access and processing through APIs. It allows users and applications to request and process relevant portions of Earth Observation data rather than systematically retrieving complete source products.
    openEO openEO provides a standardised interface for processing Earth Observation data in cloud environments. It enables users to execute processing workflows closer to where the data is stored.
    Jupyter-based environments Jupyter environments provide interactive computing capabilities for data exploration, analysis and the development of processing workflows.
    On-Demand Production On-Demand Production enables selected products to be generated in response to user requirements rather than relying exclusively on pre-generated outputs.

    These services support different user needs and should not be understood as mutually exclusive. A single user journey may combine visual exploration, server-side processing, targeted downloads and subsequent local analysis.

    Main Missions and Products

    The environmental assessment covers products associated with the main Copernicus Sentinel missions available through CDSE:

    • Sentinel-1, providing radar-based observations;
    • Sentinel-2, providing multispectral optical observations;
    • Sentinel-3, supporting land and ocean environmental monitoring;
    • Sentinel-5P, providing atmospheric composition and air-quality observations.

    The assessment scope also includes:

    • Copernicus Land Monitoring Service products;
    • Copernicus Digital Elevation Model products;
    • selected Copernicus Contributing Missions products.

    Different products have different file sizes, processing requirements and usage frequencies. The environmental impact of a product therefore depends not only on its technical characteristics, but also on the number of users and the way in which the product is accessed, processed and retained.

    User Journeys

    A CDSE user journey can extend across several successive stages.

    # Usage stage Usage description
    1 Discover The user searches catalogues, consults metadata or identifies relevant products.
    2 Explore The user previews or visualises data to assess suitability.
    3 Access The user streams, downloads or retrieves the required dataset or subset.
    4 Process The user applies algorithms either through CDSE services or within a local or external environment.
    5 Store Source data, intermediate files or final results may be retained.
    6 Share and reuse Data or derived outputs may be shared with colleagues, clients, students, public platforms or other downstream users.

    For assessment purposes, direct CDSE interactions were organised into three main activity types: streaming, downloading and computing.

    End-users’ typologies

    The study covers seven categories of CDSE end-users. These categories provide a consistent basis for survey analysis, interviews, environmental modelling and user-specific recommendations.

    End-user typologies End-users’ description
    Research and Education This category includes research and educational organisations using Copernicus data for scientific studies, teaching, experimentation and innovation. Research and Education represented the largest category in the completed survey sample.
    National, Regional and Local Public Authorities Public authorities may use Copernicus data to support public policies, territorial management, environmental monitoring, planning and operational decision-making.
    International Public Organisations International public organisations use Earth Observation data for activities conducted across countries or regions, including policy support and operational programmes.
    Charities and NGOs Charities and non-governmental organisations may use Copernicus data to support environmental, humanitarian or public-interest activities.
    Natural Persons and Personal Interest Users This category includes individuals accessing CDSE for personal exploration, learning or other non-organisational purposes.
    Business, Commercial and Professional Users – SMEs This group covers small and medium-sized organisations using Copernicus data for commercial or professional applications.
    Business, Commercial and Professional Users – non-SMEs This category covers larger commercial and professional organisations. It is considered separately from SMEs to allow different organisational contexts and usage patterns to be analysed.

    2. Study Methodology

    Understanding the environmental impact of CDSE data use requires going beyond traditional platform statistics and infrastructure metrics. While operational indicators provide valuable information on data volumes, service usage and user activity, they do not fully capture what happens once data leaves the platform.

    To address this limitation, the study developed a dedicated methodology combining quantitative and qualitative data collection, environmental modelling and lifecycle assessment principles. The objective was to reconstruct representative end-user journeys and quantify the environmental impacts associated with the complete use of Copernicus data.

    2.1. Assessment scope

    a. System Boundaries

    The assessment focuses on the environmental impacts generated by CDSE end-users throughout the lifecycle of their interactions with Copernicus data.

    The scope includes:

    • Accessing and visualising EO data through CDSE services;
    • Downloading products or subsets;
    • Processing datasets within CDSE services;
    • Processing datasets on local or external infrastructures;
    • Storing downloaded and processed data;
    • Sharing and redistributing datasets or derived products to downstream users.

    The study does not reassess satellite operations or Copernicus Ground Segment infrastructure already covered in the previous ESA assessment. Instead, it extends the analysis to the user side of the value chain.

    b. 3 levels of impact

    To capture the complete environmental footprint of data use, the methodology follows data beyond the first interaction with the platform.

    Levels Descriptions
    Level 1: Direct CDSE usage Level 1 includes all activities occurring within the CDSE ecosystem:

    - Data discovery and visualisation;
    - Streaming services;
    - Online access requests;
    - Cloud processing services;
    - On-demand production services.

    This level represents the environmental impacts directly linked to the use of CDSE services.
    Level 2: Post-download activities Level 2 captures all activities occurring after data has been transferred to the user’s own environment:

    - Local storage;
    - External cloud storage;
    - Local processing;
    - Institutional computing infrastructure;
    - Generation of intermediate and final outputs.

    This level was identified as the largest contributor to overall environmental impacts because downloaded data may be processed repeatedly and retained for long periods.
    Level 3: Redistribution impacts Many users do not consume data only for themselves. Level 3 assesses impacts generated when:

    - EO datasets are redistributed;
    - Processed outputs are shared;
    - Downstream users reuse generated products;
    - Information is transferred to secondary user communities.

    This level allows the study to account for impact amplification occurring beyond the initial user interaction.

    2.2. Data collection approach

    Because no single data source can fully describe end-user behaviour, the study combined multiple complementary sources.

    Source Descriptions
    CDSE reports Official CDSE reports provided the factual baseline for the assessment. These reports supplied information regarding:

    - Number of registered users;
    - Data volumes distributed;
    - Product catalogue characteristics;
    - Service usage statistics;
    - Platform activity indicators.

    This information was used to define representative usage patterns and support extrapolation of results to the broader CDSE community.
    Surveys A large-scale survey was developed to collect detailed information about user behaviour. The questionnaire explored:

    - User organisation type;
    - Missions and products used;
    - Access methods;
    - Processing practices;
    - Storage habits;
    - Data sharing behaviours;
    - Sustainability awareness.

    The final dataset included 1,173 completed responses covering all major CDSE user categories.

    The survey provided the quantitative backbone of the environmental assessment.
    Interviews Survey results were complemented by targeted interviews with representative users. A total of 30 one-to-one interviews were conducted across seven end-user categories.
    The interviews enabled the project team to:
    - Reconstruct complete workflows;
    - Understand decision-making processes;
    - Identify data-retention practices;
    - Assess redistribution patterns;
    - Validate survey findings;
    - Identify realistic reduction opportunities.
    This qualitative layer proved essential for understanding behaviours that are not visible through platform statistics alone.

    2.3. Calculator approach

    a. Objective

    The aim is to evaluate the comprehensive environmental impact associated with end-users’ utilisation of the CDSE portal across the full usage lifecycle. This includes assessing impacts related to data access, downloads, storage, local processing, and redistribution practices beyond the CDSE infrastructure itself. By analysing real usage patterns and end-user behaviours, this stream seeks to identify key impact drivers, quantify associated emissions, and establish a robust baseline to support targeted reduction actions and informed decision-making.

    b. Calculation approach

    The scheme below presents the updated scope of the 2025 environmental impact assessment and highlights the shift compared to the 2024 study.

    General overview on the calculation scope.

    To further clarify the scope of calculation, we present a summary of its main components below.

    • Functional unit and analytical layers: The study evaluates the environmental impacts generated by end-users’ interactions with CDSE in 2025. It expands the scope beyond the 2024 ground-segment analysis to account for streaming, downloading, and computation performed across CDSE services.

    • Geographical & temporal scope: The analysis covers all global regions where CDSE is accessed, represented on the slide across six continents. The environmental assessment is performed over a one-year period, using usage data collected between 19 January and 13 February 2026, complemented by geographic distributions and structural metrics extracted from the 2024 CDSE report. This ensures that both the temporal dynamics and the worldwide distribution of CDSE end-users are consistently reflected.

    • Assessment scope: The assessment spans the full set of CDSE services through which end-users engage with data, including the Copernicus Browser, Sentinel Hub, openEO, the S3 interface, OData, API-based downloads, On-Demand Processing, and JupyterLab. It covers a wide range of Sentinel products and Copernicus datasets, such as Sentinel-1 GRD/SLC, Sentinel-2 L1C/L2A, Sentinel-3 OLCI/SLSTR/SYN/SRAL, Sentinel-5P L1B/L2, CLMS layers, DEMs, and contributing mission data. This breadth ensures alignment with the actual diversity of usage patterns observed across the CDSE ecosystem, consistent with the approach adopted in earlier phases.

    Detailed overview on the calculation methodology.

    c. Calculation Principles

    The environmental impact assessment is based on a Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) approach combined with the Digital Services Product Category Rules (PCR) framework. Together, these methodologies make it possible to quantify the environmental impacts associated with CDSE data usage across the complete digital value chain.

    Life Cycle Assessment (LCA)

    The study evaluates both:

    • Operational impacts, generated during the use of digital services (streaming, downloading, computing and storage);

    • Embodied impacts, generated during the manufacturing of equipment and infrastructure mobilised by data usage.

    Operational impacts primarily originate from electricity consumption during data access, processing and storage, while embodied impacts are associated with the extraction of raw materials, equipment manufacturing and infrastructure deployment.

    Digital Services PCR Framework

    Following the Digital Services Product Category Rules (PCR), the environmental footprint of CDSE data usage is assessed across three technical layers:

    Layer Examples
    User Equipment Laptops, workstations and end-user devices
    Network Data transmission and connectivity infrastructure
    Digital Infrastructure Datacentres, servers, storage systems, vCPUs and GPUs

    These 3 layers are considered throughout the assessment for all user activities.

    Environmental Impact Calculation

    For each user activity, the environmental footprint is calculated as the combination of:

    Annual Environmental Impact = Embodied Impacts + Operational Impacts

    Operational impacts are estimated using activity data collected through the survey and interviews (streaming duration, downloaded volumes, processing frequency, storage duration, etc.) combined with environmental factors such as electricity mixes and infrastructure consumption.

    Embodied impacts are estimated by allocating a share of the manufacturing footprint of the equipment and infrastructure used throughout the user journey, including end-user devices, network equipment and datacentre resources.

    Scope of Application

    The methodology is applied across:

    • The three impact levels (Level 1, Level 2 and Level 3);
    • All CDSE services covered by the assessment;
    • All user typologies;
    • A one-year assessment period;
    • Six geographical regions representing the worldwide CDSE user base.

    Environmental Indicators

    The assessment quantifies four environmental indicators:

    • Greenhouse Gas Emissions (GHG) – contribution to climate change;
    • Water Consumption – freshwater use associated with digital activities;
    • Primary Energy Consumption – energy mobilised throughout the lifecycle;
    • Abiotic Resource Depletion – consumption of non-renewable materials and critical resources.

    d. Uncertainties

    The uncertainty assessment follows a structured methodology that combines the uncertainty levels of each input metric with its relative weight in the overall model. This approach makes it possible to derive a robust uncertainty estimate for each of the three impact levels.

    The results show that Level 1 carries the lowest uncertainty, as it relies mainly on well-established usage data and direct measurements from the CDSE ecosystem.

    Level 2 displays higher uncertainty, reflecting the greater reliance on assumptions for post-download behaviours such as retention duration, duplication, and local computing.

    Level 3 exhibits the highest uncertainty, largely due to the limited visibility on downstream redistribution patterns and the assumptions required to model rank-2 user behavior.

    Despite these variations, the overall uncertainty of ±22–30% remains moderate, indicating that the model provides results that are sufficiently stable and reliable to support trend analysis, cross-level comparisons and hotspot identification.

    Input category Data nature Uncertainty* Weight in the model*

    **Uncertainty categories follow the study’s methodological framework: ±5–20% indicates low uncertainty; ±20–30% reflects moderate uncertainty and acceptable variability; and ≥±30% denotes high uncertainty, where results should be interpreted as exploratory.*

    The uncertainty assigned to each input depends on the reliability and nature of its data source, while the weight in the model reflects how frequently each input is used in the impact calculation.

    Survey- and interview-based inputs act as proportional metrics and are expected to remain stable over time, meaning ESA can reuse them in future assessments with limited influence on overall uncertainty.

    3. Study Results

    The environmental assessment revealed that the impacts associated with Copernicus data use extend far beyond the direct operation of the CDSE platform. While data access services remain essential to the ecosystem, the majority of environmental impacts are generated after data has been downloaded, stored, processed and shared by end-users.

    The study highlights the importance of understanding complete user workflows rather than focusing exclusively on platform activity metrics. User decisions regarding data selection, processing environments, storage duration and redistribution practices strongly influence the overall environmental footprint.

    3.1. Key findings

    a. Main insights

    User impacts are significantly higher than previously estimated

    The 2025 assessment demonstrates that environmental impacts linked to end-users are substantially higher than the exploratory estimate included in the 2024 infrastructure assessment.

    The main reason is that the new approach incorporates actual user behaviours, including storage practices, local processing and downstream redistribution, which were previously difficult to observe.

    Environmental impacts occur mainly after data leaves CDSE

    Contrary to common assumptions, most environmental impacts are not generated during data access itself.

    The assessment shows that impacts are primarily driven by:

    • Long-term storage;
    • Local computing activities;
    • Duplication of datasets;
    • Repeated processing;
    • Redistribution to downstream users.

    User behaviour matters as much as technology

    The same dataset may generate very different environmental footprints depending on:

    • Whether data is streamed or downloaded;
    • Whether processing is performed once or repeatedly;
    • How long outputs are retained;
    • Whether data is redistributed;
    • The infrastructure used for processing.

    c. Comparison

    To facilitate interpretation of the results, the environmental impacts associated with CDSE end-user data usage have been translated into market and organizational equivalences. This approach aims to contextualize the magnitude of emissions by comparing them with familiar digital uses and with the carbon footprints of large organizations, making the results more tangible for decision-makers.

    At user-activity level, the analysis shows that a single download session generates approximately 41 gCO₂e, a streaming session around 42 gCO₂e per user, and a computing session about 48 gCO₂e. Aggregated at annual scale, level-1 end-user impacts amount to approximately 1,216 tCO₂e. This footprint is equivalent to about 86 kTB of data downloaded over one year, as well as 4 million hours of streaming and 2 million hours of computing on the CDSE portal.

    These results were also positioned against broader organizational references. ESA’s core activities represented approximately 1,100,000 tCO₂e in 2019, mainly driven by spacecraft development, launcher operations, and research and development activities. In this context, CDSE end-user data usage represents only a marginal share of overall ESA emissions. A similar pattern is observed when compared with large digital service providers: OVHcloud’s 2025 carbon footprint is largely dominated by scope 3 emissions, which account for 83% of total emissions, highlighting the critical role of downstream usage and external infrastructures.

    Likewise, Capgemini’s information system footprint in 2025 reached around 34,000 tCO₂e, primarily driven by end-user devices, illustrating again that digital environmental impacts are predominantly generated at the user level rather than by core infrastructure alone.

    Overall, these equivalences show that while the absolute carbon footprint of CDSE end-user activities remains low compared with large organizational emissions, its structure is fully consistent with wider digital ecosystems. This comparison reinforces the relevance of focusing on end-user behaviors, usage practices, and in-portal guidance as key levers to effectively reduce the environmental impact associated with CDSE data usage.

    Overview of impact – All levels

    The consolidated results across the four environmental indicators show a clear hierarchy between the three levels of end-user activity.

    images/results-by-impact-level.png

This result is consistent with the nature of Level 2 practices: once data is downloaded from CDSE, it is frequently stored for several years, often in multiple locations or duplicated copies, and sometimes reprocessed locally. These post-download behaviors generate substantial operational and embodied impacts, particularly for water consumption, primary energy and abiotic resource depletion, which are highly sensitive to long-term storage volumes and to the manufacturing footprint of user-side infrastructure.

    Level 3 represents the second largest contributor, driven by the redistribution of processed outputs to rank-2 end-users, who introduce additional streaming, downloading and storage needs. Even though the shared products are lighter than raw satellite data, the scale effect with large numbers of downstream users creates a measurable contribution across GHG emissions, water use and final energy.

    Level 1 has the smallest relative impact, reflecting the comparatively limited energy required for portal usage activities (streaming, downloading, and CDSE-based computing). These processes are short-lived and rely on optimized cloud infrastructure, leading to a footprint that is small in absolute terms.

    Across all three levels, the analysis reveals distinct impact drivers depending on the indicator. Operational emissions dominate GHG emissions and primary energy, reflecting the weight of electricity consumption during use. Conversely, abiotic resource depletion is primarily driven by embodied impacts, especially the materials and manufacturing of IT equipment mobilized in Level 2. Taken together, the results highlight that most of the environmental footprint arises after data leaves CDSE, underlining the importance of addressing storage practices, retention policies and downstream redistribution patterns to achieve meaningful reductions.

    a. Level 1 (CDSE usage)

    This view presents the environmental impacts associated solely with the direct use of the CDSE portal by rank-1 end-users, covering streaming, downloading and CDSE-based computing.

    images/results-level1-footprint.png

    Figure: Results of Level 1 end-user footprint assessment.

    Across all four indicators, the results show that streaming and downloading are the most frequent activities, but computing remains the highest contributor per session due to its greater energy intensity. This pattern is visible in the breakdown charts: for GHG emissions, computing represents roughly 39%, downloading 34%, and streaming 27% of the Level 1 footprint. A similar distribution appears for water consumption, primary energy and abiotic resource depletion, demonstrating that computing, although less frequent, has a stronger per-use impact than the two other operations.

    Most Level 1 impacts stem from electricity consumption, which explains why operational impacts dominate the results for GHG emissions, primary energy and water consumption. In contrast, abiotic resource depletion reflects a different structure: it is primarily driven by the embodied impacts of IT and network equipment manufacturing, which are mobilised every time a user streams, downloads or computes.

    Overall, the findings indicate that Level 1 offers meaningful opportunities for impact reduction. Because portal-level impacts are tied to short, repeated user actions, optimising simple behavioural patterns, such as favouring streaming for quick checks, reducing unnecessary downloads, or using server-side compute more efficiently, can generate clear and immediate reductions. This makes Level 1 a highly actionable area for improving CDSE’s environmental performance.

    b. Level 2 (post-download)

    Level 2 accounts for the largest share of the overall environmental footprint, as it captures impacts linked to post-download behaviors, notably long-term data storage and local reprocessing performed by rank-1 users. At this level, the main drivers are no longer related to how data is accessed, but to how long data is retained, how many copies are kept, and how often datasets are recomputed locally, which fundamentally alters the impact structure compared with portal-level interactions observed in Level 1.

    The breakdown of post-level-download energy consumption clearly reflects this pattern. As shown in the chart, storage alone represents approximately 87% of total post-download consumption, while local computing accounts for only 13%. This distribution highlights the disproportionate weight of storage-related practices, especially when users retain large datasets for extended periods or maintain multiple redundant copies across devices or environments.

    These behaviours generate substantial operational impacts due to the continuous energy required to keep storage infrastructures active, but also significantly increase embodied impacts, as long-term storage mobilises additional IT equipment whose manufacturing footprint must be allocated over several years.

    Across all indicators, the contribution of Level 2 is therefore driven by a mix of operational intensity (continuous electricity use for storage) and embodied intensity (manufacturing of disks, servers and network components). This is particularly visible for abiotic resource depletion, where embodied impacts dominate. Overall, Level 2 reveals structural inefficiencies in current user practices, such as uncontrolled data retention, dataset duplication, and unnecessary local recomputation. These findings make Level 2 a priority area for reduction levers, with strong potential gains through improved storage governance, retention policies, and streamlined workflows.

    Figure: Results of Level 2 end-user footprint assessment.

    d. Level 3 (redistribution)

    Level 3 impacts stem from the redistribution of processed outputs to a very large base of rank-2 end-users, which fundamentally reshapes the environmental profile compared with Levels 1 and 2. Although the shared datasets are generally lightweight, their circulation across an estimated 18 million downstream users produces a measurable footprint driven primarily by the scale of dissemination rather than the size of the files themselves. The analysis shows that operational impacts dominate for GHG emissions and primary energy, reflecting the cumulative electricity consumption associated with repeated streaming and access events on end-user devices. By contrast, embodied impacts become more prominent for abiotic resource depletion and water consumption, as the widespread mobilisation of personal devices and network equipment amplifies the manufacturing footprint of this infrastructure across millions of users. Importantly, Level 3 confirms that downstream sharing, even of compact products, can generate significant operational and embodied impacts when outputs are replicated across large audiences. This underscores the need to better understand redistribution pathways and promote efficient dissemination mechanisms, particularly since 22% of all outputs are produced for third-party use, making Level 3 an essential area for targeted reduction strategies.

    4. End-users Best Practices

    The environmental assessment identified that most impacts are generated after users access Copernicus data. Downloaded datasets are often stored for long periods, duplicated across systems, reprocessed several times and redistributed to other users.

    The objective of the following recommendations is not to reduce the value created by Earth Observation data, but to help users obtain the same benefits with fewer unnecessary environmental impacts. The most effective actions generally involve limiting unnecessary transfers, avoiding duplicate storage and reducing repeated reprocessing.

    4.1. Deep Dive by Usage Type

    a. Filter and focus before you click

    Most projects only use a fraction of a raw dataset. The fewer pixels, dates, or bands you touch, the less energy every step of the workflow consumes.

    Why It Matters

    Many workflows begin with the download of complete datasets before their relevance has been verified.

    However, CDSE offers multiple services that allow users to explore data before downloading it. Using these capabilities can significantly reduce unnecessary transfers and downstream storage requirements.

    Best practices What to do & why Impact Reduction example
    Check metadata and preview before streaming or downloading Before opening, streaming or downloading a product, review the available metadata and use quicklook previews in Copernicus Browser, Sentinel Hub or the CDSE catalogue. Check acquisition date, cloud cover, product type (e.g. Sentinel-2 L1C vs L2A), mission, spatial coverage and processing level to verify that the image is relevant before accessing full-resolution data. Medium A coastal monitoring team reviews Sentinel-3 quicklooks and metadata before opening imagery. Only 2 of 15 acquisitions are relevant for analysis, avoiding unnecessary access to unsuitable products.
    Narrow before you pull data Before streaming or downloading, constrain your request using AOI polygons, time filters, mission selection, cloud-cover filters, resolution requirements and band selection. Use STAC queries, Sentinel Hub requests or openEO filters to retrieve only the information that supports the objective. High A regional planning authority analyses urban expansion using a municipality boundary as an AOI and retrieves only the required Sentinel-2 bands and dates instead of a full tile. This can reduce handled data volumes by up to 90%.
    Filter and mask before you compute Apply cloud masks, shadow masks, Sentinel-2 Scene Classification Layer (SCL) classes, Sentinel-1 quality flags and spatial filters before launching analytics, classification, statistics or AI workflows. Processing unusable pixels consumes resources and often leads to re-runs. High A flood monitoring workflow uses Sentinel-1 imagery and excludes low-quality observations before generating flood extent maps, reducing processed pixels and avoiding recalculations.
    Use analysis-ready data whenever possible Reuse Analysis Ready Data (ARD), Sentinel mosaics, Copernicus Land Monitoring Service products, pre-computed indicators or thematic layers whenever they already provide the information required. Avoid rebuilding products that already exist and have been validated. High A land-cover project reuses CLMS Imperviousness products instead of processing dozens of Sentinel-2 acquisitions to recreate the same indicator.
    Reduction Example

    A PhD researcher studying vegetation change in a watershed needs Sentinel-2 imagery for NDVI analysis. Instead of exploring every available image, the researcher checks cloud cover, acquisition dates and previews, then filters to the study area and only the bands required (B04 and B08). By filtering before accessing data, they can avoid up to 90% of downloaded volume.

    b. Download only if necessary

    Downloads you don’t need create copies that are kept unused indefinitely. Lighter alternatives exist at every stage.

    Why It Matters

    The study identified downloading as a trigger event that often initiates years of storage and additional processing activities.

    The environmental footprint of a download therefore extends far beyond the data transfer itself.

    Best practices What to do & why Impact Reduction example
    Stream before you download When the objective is exploration, scene selection or visual validation, use Copernicus Browser, Sentinel Hub visualisation layers, WMS/WMTS services or quicklooks instead of downloading data. Download only products that will actually be analysed or reused. High A researcher compares crop conditions across multiple regions using Sentinel Hub visualisation layers and downloads only the final selected scenes.
    Process where the data lives Run computations directly within the CDSE ecosystem using openEO, Sentinel Hub Processing API, On-Demand Processing or JupyterLab. Export only final indicators, maps or statistics instead of first downloading raw EO products. Very High A water authority computes monthly vegetation indicators in openEO and exports only a CSV time series instead of downloading hundreds of Sentinel products locally.
    Check before you duplicate Before downloading or regenerating a dataset, verify whether an equivalent product already exists in an organisational repository, previous project or shared EO workspace. Reuse validated mosaics, AI-ready datasets and derived products whenever possible. Very High A biodiversity project reuses an existing Sentinel-2 mosaic stored in the organisation’s EO repository instead of generating and storing another copy.
    Use a single source of truth Store datasets once and provide access through a shared repository, data catalogue, object store or geospatial platform. Share references or links rather than distributing copies across teams and projects. Very High Five departments access the same flood-risk dataset through a shared repository rather than maintaining separate project copies.
    Download derived products instead of raw data Where possible, export zonal statistics, indicators, dashboards, classified maps, AI-ready features or reporting products rather than downloading source imagery. Many use cases require information, not raw EO files. Very High A forestry analyst exports annual biomass statistics instead of storing the complete Sentinel-2 archive used to produce them.
    Reduction Example

    A regional authority monitoring flood risk needs updated flood extent maps. Instead of downloading hundreds of GB of Sentinel archives to process locally, the team reviews imagery in Copernicus Browser, generates NDWI time series, zonal statistics or flood extent layers directly in CDSE using cloud processing services, and exports only the final outputs needed for decision-making. If they download a 10 GB AOI-based subset instead of a 100 GB full scene, it reduces the volume handled throughout the workflow by up to 90% (downloaded, stored and processed data volumes).

    c. Handle the result, not the whole dataset

    The outputs you need are often thousands of times lighter than raw Earth Observation data. Working with these means far less data flowing through every step of the lifecycle.

    Why It Matters

    More than 80% of environmental impacts are associated with storage and local reprocessing activities.

    Many users keep large volumes of source data even when only derived products remain useful.

    Best practices What to do & why Impact Reduction example
    Keep the outputs, drop the raw data Once analyses are completed, retain only the products needed for operations, reproducibility, compliance or reuse. Store final maps, dashboards, indicators, models and reports rather than all raw Sentinel-1 or Sentinel-2 products used to generate them when those products remain available from CDSE. Very High A national forestry programme keeps annual forest-cover indicators and reports while removing local copies of the Sentinel imagery used to create them.
    Archive what’s inactive and set a retention period Apply storage lifecycle management. Keep active datasets on operational storage, archive completed projects and automatically remove temporary artefacts such as cloud masks, testing outputs or intermediate mosaics according to predefined retention rules. Very High An EO analytics project automatically removes temporary cloud masks, testing outputs and intermediate mosaics after 3 months, while archiving completed projects.
    Share only what your audience needs Adapt what you distribute to the needs of the recipient. Decision-makers often need dashboards, KPIs, web maps, PDFs or indicators rather than raw EO datasets. Sharing lighter outputs reduces duplication and downstream processing. Very High A climate adaptation programme shares an online dashboard and web map service with decision-makers instead of distributing underlying EO datasets.
    Share your process along with your output Publish openEO Process Graphs, GitHub repositories, Jupyter notebooks, Python/R workflows, processing parameters, metadata and source product identifiers alongside results so that others can reproduce analyses without exchanging large intermediate datasets. Very High A research consortium publishes an openEO workflow and GitHub repository rather than exchanging 50 GB of intermediate processing outputs.
    Share references instead of copies Use STAC Items, catalogue references, cloud object links, dataset identifiers or API endpoints rather than creating additional copies of the same dataset. This preserves traceability and limits duplication. Very High Ten users access the same Sentinel-2 dataset through a shared STAC reference instead of downloading ten separate copies.
    Reduction Example

    An international development NGO monitoring crop yields across regions processes Sentinel data to generate yield maps. They retain the final indicators and forecasts for partner organisations, archive completed growing seasons, and publish their analysis methodology, avoiding the need to store or share raw satellite data. Publishing the workflow also allows colleagues to reproduce the analysis without storing or exchanging large intermediate datasets.

    Key Takeaway

    The most effective environmental reduction strategies are not necessarily technical. They often involve better decisions regarding data selection, downloading, storage duration, processing frequency and collaboration practices. By adopting these best practices, users can significantly reduce avoidable impacts while preserving the scientific, operational and societal value of Copernicus data.

    5. CDSE Future Improvements

    5.1. Prioritised Reduction Levers

    The reduction framework is structured around 3 complementary families of levers.

    • Direct reduction through tooling focuses on immediate impact cuts enabled by new platform features such as impact display, smarter dataset recommendations, improved previews, and automated compute optimization.

    • Reduction driven by end user behavior aims to guide users toward lower impact practices through awareness sessions, communication campaigns, improved documentation and nudges that influence usage, storage and processing habits.

    • Data & insights refinement strengthens ESA’s long-term reduction strategy by deepening Level-2 and Level-3 analyses, evaluating avoided emissions, and improving the understanding of hotspots to better target future optimizations.

    Figure: Synthesis of prioritised reduction levers.

    The prioritized Level 1 levers resulting from Stream 4 do not pre-empt implementation; they provide a rank-ordered, evidence-based basis for ESA/CDSE to consider feasibility checks, design options, and sequencing in a subsequent step outside the scope of this study.

    The most important levers have been selected and organised into a clear multi-year roadmap. The plan is structured in three stages: building the foundations in 2026–2027, scaling and systematising reductions in 2028–2029, and transforming the broader ecosystem from 2030 onwards. This phased approach ensures that actions are prioritized, sequenced and implemented progressively to maximise impact and enable smooth long-term adoption.

    5.2. Long-term Vision (2030+)

    The analysis shows that future GHG emissions do not decrease uniformly across scenarios. Instead, strategic choices redirect the footprint between lifecycle components (compute, storage, data transmission and end-users). Scenarios that prioritize compute, redistribution architecture or behavioral change each generate a different emissions profile by 2035. This confirms that CGS’s footprint is highly sensitive to where the intervention occurs rather than to the magnitude of the intervention alone.

    images/ghg-emissions-scenarios-2035.png

    Figure 54: GHG emissions: As is 2023 vs Scenarios by 2035.

    a. Scenario 1: Without action, the CGS footprint could nearly double

    The long-term trajectory (Scenario 1) indicates that GHG emissions could almost double by 2035 if no additional actions are implemented. This rise is driven by structural growth in storage volumes, compute demand and data transmission factors that mechanically grow with mission expansion and increased EO data use.

    b. Scenario 2: Expanding compute increases embodied emissions

    Scenario 2 highlights a critical trade-off: shifting workloads to on-platform compute reduces downloads but significantly increases embodied emissions. Additional infrastructure, servers, GPUs and networking hardware, must be manufactured and maintained, which raises the upstream environmental burden. This scenario underscores the need to consider the full lifecycle impact of expanding compute capacity, not only operational energy.

    c. Scenario 3: Supplier commitments are the only structural lever on infrastructure emissions

    This scenario demonstrates that supplier-driven improvements, notably better datacenter efficiency (lower PUE) and electricity decarbonization, represent the only pathway capable of structurally constraining infrastructure-related emissions. These interventions directly reduce operational emissions associated with compute, storage and transmission, making supplier engagement a critical strategic lever for long-term mitigation.

    d. Scenario 4: Responsible end-user behaviour reduces downstream emissions

    This scenario shows that end-user commitment plays a decisive role in lowering emissions at the user-side levels (L1/L2). Reducing unnecessary downloads, avoiding duplication and shortening storage duration all contribute to lowering the downstream footprint. Behaviour-driven scenarios therefore offer substantial potential for reducing Level 1 and Level 2 emissions through improved practices and clearer guidance.

    e. Scenario 5: Redistribution hubs shift emissions toward data transmission

    This Scenario 5, which deploys a network of redistribution hubs, reduces long-distance user access but shifts part of the footprint toward data transmission. Maintaining mirrored datasets across regions increases network-related emissions, even though it improves access performance and reduces user-side impacts. This scenario illustrates that architectural choices can redistribute emissions rather than reduce them.

    6.4. FAQ

    Question Answer
    Why was this assessment conducted? The study was conducted to better understand how Copernicus data is used after access and to identify the activities that contribute most to environmental impacts. The objective is to support informed decisions and identify practical reduction opportunities across the CDSE ecosystem.
    Does using Copernicus data create significant environmental impacts? Yes, like any digital service, Copernicus data usage relies on devices, networks and digital infrastructure. However, the study shows that most impacts are not generated during data access itself, but after download through storage, local processing and redistribution activities.
    Why are post-download activities more impactful than CDSE usage? Once data leaves the platform, users may store it for years, create multiple copies, process it repeatedly or share it with additional users. These activities generate impacts that can significantly exceed those associated with the initial access to data.
    Why is data storage such an important hotspot? Storage infrastructures consume resources throughout the entire retention period. Long-term retention, duplicated datasets and inactive archives can generate impacts long after the original download has taken place.
    Does the study recommend downloading less data? Not necessarily. The objective is to access the right data in the most efficient way. In many cases, streaming, filtering, cloud processing or downloading only relevant subsets can deliver the same value with lower environmental impacts.
    Are all users equally impactful? No. Impacts vary according to the type of activity performed, the amount of data handled, storage duration, processing intensity and redistribution practices. Large user communities and intensive workflows generally generate higher overall impacts.
    Can environmental impacts be reduced without reducing the value of Copernicus data? Yes. The assessment identified several best practices that can significantly reduce avoidable impacts while preserving scientific, operational and societal benefits. Examples include filtering data before access, processing closer to the data, reducing duplication and applying retention policies.
